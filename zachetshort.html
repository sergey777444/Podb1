3. Subject-verb agreement

Definition

The subject and the verb must agree in person and number.

  • Singular subject → singular verb.
  • Plural subject → plural verb.

10 rules

1
Prepositional phrases do not affect the verb.
The study of languages is interesting.
2
AND takes a plural verb.
John and Mary are friends.
3
Indefinite pronouns usually take singular verbs.
Everybody works.
4
Either…or / Neither…nor agree with the second subject.
Either he or she is right.
5
Always plural nouns take plural verbs.
Trousers are expensive.
6
There is / There are agree with the following noun.
There are two books.
7
Who agrees with its antecedent.
It is I who am right.
8
Time, distance, money take singular verbs when considered as one unit.
Three miles is far.
9
Expressions of portion agree with the noun after OF.
A lot of water is needed. / A lot of students are here.
10
Collective nouns — singular or plural depending on meaning.
My family has/have arrived.

4. Nouns

1. Types of nouns

  • Proper — a particular person, place or thing: Michael, Paris, India.
  • Common — a class of objects or a concept: chair, milk, atmosphere.
  • Pair — things made of two identical parts: jeans, scissors, pajamas.
  • Possessive — ownership: Lisa’s bag, mother’s dress.
  • Compound — two or more existing words: rocking-horse, father-in-law, passer-by.

2. Both countable and uncountable

The countable meaning is often more specific, the uncountable one more general.

CountableUncountable
a glass — стаканsome glass — стекло
a coffee — чашка кофеsome coffee — кофе
a hair — волосокsome hair — волосы
a room — комнатаsome room — место, пространство

3. Always plural nouns

Have only a plural form and take a plural verb: arms, clothes, jeans, scissors.

My clothes are beautiful. / These scissors are sharp. / His jeans are black.

5. Determiners (I)

1. Some / Any

Some — positive sentences: I have some rice.

Any — negatives and questions; in positive sentences means «любой»: I haven’t got any money. / Are there any mushrooms? / You can take any bus.

2. A lot of / Lots of / Much / Many

  • A lot of / Lots of — large quantity, often in statements: I have a lot of friends.
  • Much — uncountable; questions, negatives, after too / so / as: I don’t have much time. / There is too much sugar.
  • Many — countable; questions, negatives, after too / so / as: How many books do you have?

3. A few / Few / A little / Little

DeterminerUsageExample
A fewCountable, positivea few friends
FewCountable, negativefew people
A littleUncountable, positivea little money
LittleUncountable, negativelittle water

4. All / All of

All — before plural or uncountable nouns: All children. / All the apples.

All of — before pronouns and nouns with determiners (of can be omitted before a noun, but not before a pronoun): All of them. / All of the children.

5. Half / Half of

Half — one of two equal parts: Half the cake.

Half of — before pronouns and nouns with determiners: Half of them. / Half of the cake.

6. Determiners (II)

Most

Before a plural or uncountable noun; most of for particular things; can be used alone.

Most trees have leaves. / Most of the students are ready. / Most are boring.

No / None

No — before a noun; none — with of or alone.

There is no water. / None of us is ready. / There is none left.

Every / Each

Every — a whole group (usually 3+); each — individuals one by one. Both before singular nouns. Each can be used alone, with of, in mid position or after a pronoun.

Every person supported the party. / Each of them was ready. / She called them each.

Whole

Before a singular noun; all or complete.

We spent the whole night dancing.

Both

Two things: the one and the other. Forms: both, both the, both of the, both of them; alone or after a pronoun.

Both of them are ready. / The laws both concern the social sphere.

Either / Neither

Either — the one or the other; neither — not the one or the other. For two things; alone or with of.

Either of them is fine. / Neither of the dresses matches these shoes.

8. Questions

1. Yes/No (closed)

  • Begin with an auxiliary or modal verb.
  • Answered by yes or no.
  • Short answer: yes/no + pronoun + auxiliary.
Are they playing? — Yes, they are.

2. Wh- (open)

  • Begin with what, where, when, why, how, which, whose.
  • Unlimited answers.
  • What — things; which — limited choice.
  • How can stand alone or before an adjective/adverb.
Where are you going?

3. Negative

  • Use a negative auxiliary (full or contracted).
  • Ask for confirmation, annoyance, admiration or surprise.
Didn’t Tim prepare everything?

4. Alternative

  • Express a choice between two or more possibilities.
  • Often use OR.
Would you like tea or coffee?

5. Subject

  • Ask about the subject.
  • Begin with who, what or which.
  • No do/does/did.
Who works here?

6. Object

  • Ask about the object.
  • Structure: question word + auxiliary + subject + verb.
What does John play?

7. Tag

  • Statement + auxiliary + pronoun at the end.
  • Positive statement → negative tag; negative → positive tag.
  • They refers to somebody/everybody; it refers to nothing.
She is nice, isn’t she?

8. Indirect

  • Introduced by Do you know…? / Can you tell me…? / I wonder…
  • Word order as in statements: subject + verb.
Do you know where she lives?

10. Adverbs

1. Position in the sentence

Three positions: Front — at the beginning; Mid — before the main verb, but after to be; End — after the main verb or direct object.

TypePosition
FrequencyUsually mid; sometimes front or end
TimeUsually front; soon/now can be mid
PlaceUsually end
MannerUsually end; -ly adverbs can sometimes be mid
Sentence adverbsFront, mid or end
AlsoMid
Too / As wellEnd

Important

  • Still — mid.
  • Yet — end (negative/question).
  • No longer — mid.
  • Any longer / any more — end.
She usually travels by car. / He quietly entered the room. / He drives a car too.
