Parts of speech: classifications and definitions

The classifications of parts of speech. The definitions of parts of speech.

Definition

A part of speech is one of the main grammatical groups that a word belongs to according to the way it is used in a sentence. Words fall into classes by meaning, morphological characteristics and syntactical functions.

Two classifications

John Eastwood (8 classes)General classification (9 classes)
verb, noun, adjective, adverb, preposition, determiner, pronoun, linking wordnoun, adjective, pronoun, numeral, verb, adverb, preposition, conjunction, interjection

Definitions

Part of speechDefinitionExamples
Nouna word (other than a pronoun) used to identify a class of people, places or things (common noun), or to name a particular one (proper noun)an actor, Emma, philosophy
Verba word describing an action, state or occurrence; forms the main part of the predicateto look, to hand, to live
Adjectivea word that tells more about a thing: appearance, colour, size or other qualitieswarm, excellent, slippery
Adverba word or phrase that modifies an adjective, verb or another adverb; expresses place, time, circumstance, manner, cause, degreetomorrow, afterwards, surprisingly
Pronouna word used instead of a noun group to refer to a person, group or thing already mentioned or obvious from the contextshe, us, somebody
Numerala word expressing a numbertwelve, five, one hundred
Prepositiona word governing (and usually preceding) a noun or pronoun, expressing its relation to another word or element in the clausein, on, at, in spite of
Conjunctiona word used to connect clauses or sentences or to coordinate words in the same clauseor, and, while
Interjectionan exclamation, especially as a part of speechOh! Wow! Hurray!
Determinera word used before a noun to show which thing(s) you are talking about (articles, possessives, demonstratives, interrogatives)that, my, the
Linking worda word that shows relationships between ideas; joins two or more sentences or clausesin spite of, although, so that

Parts of the sentence

The parts of the sentence. Their definitions.

Sentence

A sentence is the smallest unit of speech conveying a thought and built according to the laws of grammar. The most common pattern has the direct word order: subject – predicate verb – object/complement – modifier.

SubjectPredicateObject / ComplementAdverbial modifier
Mary
(a single noun)		bought
(a single verb)		some flowers
(noun phrase; object)		yesterday
(an adverb)
We
(a pronoun)		are dancing
(a verb phrase)		the tango
(a noun; object)		—
PartDefinition
Subjectthe part of the clause referring to the person or thing that does or causes the action of the verb. Usually a noun, noun group or pronoun; comes before the verb.
Predicatefollows the subject and shows the action of the sentence. Every sentence must have a verb. It may be a single verb or a verb phrase.
Objectrefers to the person or thing affected by the action of the verb; a pronoun, noun or noun phrase. Direct – directly involved in or affected by the action; indirect – receives something from or benefits from the action.
Complementfollows link verbs (be, seem, appear, become, get, feel, look, stay, sound), which cannot be followed by an object. Completes the sense of the sentence by telling something about the subject; expressed by an adjective, noun, noun phrase or pronoun.
Adverbial modifiertells the time, place and manner of the action (answers When? Where? How?); an adverb, adverbial phrase or prepositional phrase. Not every sentence needs one.

e.g. Mary has showed me (indirect object) her house (direct object).

3

Subject-verb agreement

The rules of subject-verb agreement.

Main rule

The subject and the predicate must agree in person, number and case. A singular subject takes a singular verb, a plural subject takes a plural verb.

#RuleExample
1Prepositional phrase between subject and verb has no effect on the verb.The study of the languages is very interesting.
2Two subjects joined by and → plural verb.The actress and her manager are going to a party.
3somebody, anything, nowhere, each, everybody, none of, or + singular nouns; either / neither used without or, nor → singular verb.Everybody works harder than him. If either of you takes a vacation…
4neither…nor, either…or, not only…but also, or → the verb agrees with the second subject.Neither John nor his friends are going. Either he or she is doing this job.
5Nouns that are always plural → plural verb.The trousers are on the chair.
6There is / There are agrees with the first following noun.There is a table, two chairs in the room.
7After the pronoun who the verb agrees with its antecedent.It is I who am right. It is they who are right.
8Distances, periods of time, sums of money, etc. taken as a unit → singular verb (but a plural verb if they are separate items).Three miles is too far to walk. / Ten dollars (bills) were scattered on the floor.
9Words that indicate portions (a lot, a majority, some, all, most, several, a third) → guided by the noun after of.A lot of the pie has disappeared. A lot of the pies have disappeared.
10Collective nouns (group, jury, family, audience, population) → singular or plural, depending on the writer’s intent.All of my family has / have arrived.

Nouns

Types of nouns. Always plural nouns. Nouns that can be both countable and uncountable.

Definition

Nouns name specific things, places, people, animals, feelings. Categories: number (singular, plural), case (common, possessive), gender (masculine, feminine, neutral).

Types of nouns

TypeDefinitionExamples
Properdenote a particular person, place or thingMichael, Paris, India
Commondenote a class of objects or a concept (not a particular individual)a chair, milk, atmosphere
Countablethings we can count; singular and plurala car, a bear, a house
Uncountablethings that cannot be counted; singular verbsugar, air, humidity
Pairthings made of two identical partsjeans, scissors, pajamas
Group (collective)a group of individualsaudience, family, staff
Possessiveindicate ownershipLisa’s bag, my mother’s dress
Compoundmade up of two or more existing wordsa rocking-horse, a father-in-law, a passer-by

Countable and uncountable

  • Countable nouns: a/one desk, two desks. We never leave a singular countable noun without a determiner.
  • Uncountable nouns have neither singular nor plural: air / some air, but not an air, two airs. Groups: gas, liquid, material (air, water, iron, paper); food (rice, sugar, flour, coffee); abstract ideas (health, time, fun, peace); feelings (anger, pity, courage, boredom); activities (travel, work, research, behaviour); others (advice, information, luggage, news, furniture, money, weather…).
  • Uncountable nouns ending in –s take a singular verb: acoustics, darts, economics, gymnastics, linguistics, mathematics, measles, news, physics, politics, statistics.

Always plural nouns

Have only a plural form and take a plural verb: arms (weapons), belongings, clothes, congratulations, contents, customs, earnings, goods, outskirts, remains, surroundings, thanks, troops (also pair nouns: trousers, jeans, scissors).

Nouns that can be both countable and uncountable

Often the countable noun is more specific, the uncountable one more general; sometimes the meanings differ.

NounCountableUncountable

papera paper (a newspaper)some paper (material)
glassa glass (a container)some glass (material)

timea time (one time, two times)some time
experiencean experience (something that happens to you); experiences (adventures)some experience

spacea space (an empty area between things)some space

Plural forms to remember

  • Rules: most nouns + –s; after –s, –ss, –sh, –ch, –x, –o + –es (tomato → tomatoes; but videos, photos, pianos); consonant + y → –ies (study → studies; but play → plays); –f/–fe → –ves (shelf → shelves; but roofs, chiefs, safes).
  • Irregular: man–men, woman–women, person–people, foot–feet, ox–oxen, tooth–teeth, louse–lice, mouse–mice, child–children, goose–geese.
  • Same form in singular and plural: deer, sheep, aircraft, spacecraft, species, series, crossroads, means…; only in some meanings plural: fish(es), fruit(s), salmon(s), trout(s) (fruits = different kinds).

Determiners (I)

some/any, a lot of/lots of, much/many, (a) few, (a) little, all, half.

DeterminerUseExample
some / anybefore plural or uncountable nouns. Some – in positive sentences, any – in negative sentences and questions (same for somebody, anything…). Any can be used in a positive sentence, usually with if, meaning “it doesn’t matter which”.They have got some rice. Are there any mushrooms? You can take any bus that goes to the centre.
a lot of / lots of, much / manyall mean a large quantity. A lot of / lots of – preferable in positive sentences. Much (uncountable) and many (countable) – mainly in negative sentences and questions; used after too, so, as (a lot of is not). In formal English much/many may be used in positive sentences.How many apples were there? We haven’t got much time.
(a) few / (a) littlea small quantity. With the article a – positive meaning; without it – negative meaning (hardly any). Few – countable, little – uncountable.A few friends came. / Few tourists come here.
allbefore a plural or uncountable noun. All (of) for particular things; of can be dropped after all but not before a pronoun. Can stand in mid position or after a pronoun. With a clause = “everything / the only thing”. Not used alone (compare: All the questions were solved / Everything was solved).All children must study. The apples are all ripe. All he did was hide.
halfHalf (of) with particular things or a restricted amount; of can be left out after half but not before a pronoun.Half (of) the cake…
6

Determiners (II)

most, no/none, every/each, whole, both, either/neither.

DeterminerUseExample
mostbefore a plural or uncountable noun; most of for particular things; can be used on its own.Most trees have leaves. Some are interesting, but most are boring.
no / noneNo + a noun. None – with of or on its own.There is no water. None of us is ready. There is none left.
every / eachbefore a singular noun, speaking about a whole group. Every – three or more, usually a large number (“all the people, everyone”). Each – people seen as individuals, one by one; smaller groups; can mean only two. Each (not every) can be used on its own, with of, in mid position or after a pronoun.Every person supported the party. Each person signed the agreement. She called them each.
wholebefore a singular noun; means “all” or “complete”.We spent the whole night dancing.
bothfor two things: “the one and the other”. Both / both the / both of the / both of them. Mid position or after a pronoun; can be used alone.The laws both concern the social sphere.
either / neitherfor two things. Either = “the one or the other”; neither = “not the one or the other”. Can be used alone or with of.He hasn’t answered either question. Neither of the dresses matches these shoes.

Types and position in the sentence

TypeUse and positionExample
Personalrefer to yourself, the people you talk to, or the people/things you talk about. Subject pronouns stand before the verb; object pronouns after the verb or a preposition (also in short answers and after as, than). You = people in general; they = the authorities.Me too. Adjectives

Order and degrees of comparison.

Definition

Adjectives describe nouns. They have one form: they do not change for singular/plural or masculine/feminine.

Position

  • before a noun: a wonderful girl;
  • after link verbs (be, become, seem, appear, look, feel, sound, taste, smell): He’s become difficult to talk to;
  • after get / make / keep / find + object: We found this task rather easy.
  • only before a noun: main, indoor, only, former, chief, elder, eldest, inner, outdoor, outer, principal, upper;
  • only after a linking verb: asleep, alike, pleased, ill, afraid, alone, ashamed, awake, alive, content, glad, unwell, well;
  • the + adjective = groups of people: the rich, the poor, the unemployed, the blind, the deaf, the sick, the elderly, the young.

Order of adjectives

When there are several adjectives before a noun, the order is:

Degrees of comparison

Only qualitative (gradable) adjectives have degrees: they take very, extremely. Classifying adjectives (daily, western) are non-gradable and take really, absolutely.

Patterns of comparison

  • both forms possible: clever, common, cruel, gentle, narrow, pleasant, polite, quiet, simple, stupid (gentle – gentler / more gentle – the gentlest / most gentle);
  • older (general) / elder (family members, only before a noun: my elder sister);
  • than after a comparative: more polite than their neighbours; than + object pronoun = than + subject + verb: shorter than me / than I am;
  • the + superlative + in / of: the eldest son in the family;
  • as … as (equal), not so/as … as (unequal), the same as;
  • more + noun phrase: Sport is more a way of life than a hobby; more than before adjectives for emphasis: more than satisfied;
  • two comparatives together = continuous change: better and better, more and more difficult;
  • the taller of which – the + comparative + of + noun phrase (two things);
  • The faster you run, the healthier you become – two contrasted comparatives with the.

Adverbs

Order and degrees of comparison.

Definition

Adverbs are used with verbs, adjectives or other adverbs and tell us how, when, where, how often: Tim was crawling slowly. The room is incredibly wonderful.

Formation

  • adjective + –ly: nice – nicely (but true – truly, whole – wholly); –y → –ily: heavy – heavily; –le → –ly: possible – possibly; –ic → –ically: dramatic – dramatically;
  • adjectives ending in –ly: friendly, lively, lovely, elderly, likely, lonely, silly, ugly;
  • same form as the adjective: early, fast, hard, high, late, low, right, wrong, deep, long, near, straight;
  • two adverbs with different meanings: free (without paying) – freely (uncontrolled); hard (difficult) – hardly (almost not); high – highly (very); late – lately (recently); near – nearly (almost); wide – widely (in many places);
  • good is an adjective, well an adverb; but well also describes health: He is not well (= ill).

Position of adverbs in the sentence

Three positions: front (at the very beginning), mid (before the main verb, but after to be), end (after the main verb, or after the direct object if there is one).

TypePosition and example

Degrees of comparison

  • Adverbs have degrees of comparison like adjectives: short adverbs add –er / –est (fast – faster – the fastest), longer ones use more / most (fluently – more fluently – the most fluently); the same patterns (than, as … as, the + comparative…) apply.
  • Irregular: well – better – (the) best; badly – worse – (the) worst; far – farther / further – (the) farthest / furthest; little – less – (the) least.
