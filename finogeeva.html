Introduction

Most people who study and use a language are interested in how they can do things with the language – how they can make meanings, get attention, influence people, and learn about the world. They are interested in the grammatical structure of the language as a way of getting things done. A grammar that puts together the patterns of the language and the things you can do with them is called a functional grammar. This is a functional grammar for intermediate students; that is to say, it is based on the important relation between structure and function. Grammar is the set of rules that describe the structure of a language and control the way the sentences are formed.

Noun is a word (other than a pronoun) used to identify any of a class of people, places, or things (common noun), or to name a particular one of these (proper noun). e.g. an actor, Emma, philosophy.

Verb is a word used to describe an action, state, or occurrence, and forming the main part of the predicate of a sentence, such as hear, become, happen. e.g. to look, to hand, to live.

Adjective is a word used to tell you more about a thing, such as its appearance, colour, size, or other qualities. e.g. warm, excellent, slippery.

Adverb is a word or phrase that modifies or qualifies an adjective, verb, or other adverb or a word group, expressing a relation of place, time, circumstance, manner, cause, degree. e.g. tomorrow, afterwards, surprisingly.

Pronoun is a word used instead of a noun group for referring to a person, group, or thing that has already been mentioned or that is obvious from the context. e.g. she, us, somebody.

Numeral is a word expressing a number. e.g. twelve, five, one hundred.

Preposition is a word governing, and usually preceding, a noun or pronoun and expressing a relation to another word or element in the clause. e.g. in, on, at, in spite of.

Conjunction is a word used to connect clauses or sentences or to coordinate words in the same clause. e.g. or, and, while.

Interjection is an exclamation, especially as a part of speech. e.g. Oh! Wow! Hurray!

Determiner is a word used before a noun for showing which thing or things you are talking about (articles, possessives, demonstratives, interrogatives). e.g. that, my, the.

Linking word is a word used to show relationships between ideas. It can be used to join 2 or more sentences or clauses (a clause is a group of words which contains a subject and a verb). e.g. in spite of, although, so that.

The sentence. Parts of the sentence.

A sentence is the smallest unit of speech conveying a thought and built in accordance with the laws of grammar. The most commonly used sentence pattern in English has the subject in the first position, predicate verb in the second (the direct word order), the object/complement in the third and the next modifier.

The subject is the part of the clause referring to the person or thing that does or causes the action of a verb. In English the subject is usually a single noun, noun group or a pronoun, and comes before the verb.

The predicate follows the subject: it generally shows the actions of the sentence. Every sentence must have a verb. The predicate may be a single verb, or a verb phrase.

The object is the part of the clause referring to the person or thing that is affected by the action of a verb. In English, the object is usually a pronoun, a noun or a noun phrase. Objects can be direct (directly involved in or affected by the action of the verb) and indirect (receive smth from or benefits from the action) ones. For example, Mary has showed me her house. ‘Her house’ is a direct object and ‘me’ is an indirect object.

The complement follows link verbs, such as be, seem, appear, become, get, feel, look, seem, stay, sound, which cannot be followed by an object. It completes the sense of a sentence by telling us something about the subject. It is expressed by an adjective, noun, noun phrase or a pronoun.

The adverbial modifier tells us the time, place and manner of the action. It can be an adverb, adverbial phrase or prepositional phrase. A modifier answers the question When? Where? How? Every sentence does not necessarily require a modifier.

2Section IISubject-verb agreement

Subject-verb agreement means that the subject and the predicate must agree in person, number and case. When a writer uses a singular noun, he must use a verb that is conjugated to match singular nouns. When a writer uses a plural verb, he must use a verb that is conjugated to match plural nouns.

e.g. The actress lives here. The actresses live here.

1. If the subject and verb are separated by a prepositional phrase, the prepositional phrase has no effect on the verb.

e.g. The study of the languages is very interesting.

2. If two subjects are used with the conjunction and the verb is in the plural form.

e.g. The actress and her manager are going to a party tonight.

3. Such words as somebody, anything, nowhere, each, everybody, none of, or (with singular nouns) and either, neither when used without or, nor are followed by a singular predicate.

e.g. Everybody works harder than him.

If either of you takes a vacation, you won't finish the work.

4. If two subjects are connected by neither…nor…, either...or…, not only…but also…,or the form of the verb depends on the second subject.

e.g. Neither John nor his friends are going to the beach.

Either he or she is doing this job.

5. Nouns that are always plural are followed by a plural verb.

e.g. The trousers are on the chair.

6. There is/There are agrees with the first following noun.

e.g. There is a table, two chairs in the room.

7. The verb following a pronoun who agrees with its antecedent.

e.g. It is I who am right.

It is they who are right.

8. Use a singular verb with distances, periods of time, sums of money, etc., when considered as a unit.

e.g. Three miles is too far to walk.
Five years is the maximum sentence for that offense.
Ten dollars is a high price to pay.
BUT
Ten dollars (e.g., dollar bills) were scattered on the floor.

9. With words that indicate portions such as a lot, a majority, some, all, most, several we are guided by the noun after of.

e.g. A lot of the pie has disappeared.

A lot of the pies have disappeared.

A third of the city is unemployed.

A third of the people are unemployed.

10. With collective nouns such as group, jury, family, audience, population, the verb might be singular or plural, depending on the writer's intent.

e.g. All of my family has arrived/have arrived.
Most of the jury is here/are here.

3Section IIINouns

Nouns are words that function as the names of some specific things, places, people, animals, feelings, etc. The class of nouns is constituted by the following grammatical categories: number (singular, plural), case (common, possessive), gender (masculine, feminine, neutral).

Types of nouns

Proper nouns (or names) are nouns that are used to denote a particular person, place, or thing. e.g. Michael, Paris, India.

Common nouns are nouns that denote a class of objects or a concept as opposed to a particular individual. e.g. a chair, milk, atmosphere.

Countable nouns are nouns that denote things we can count. They can be singular or plural. e.g. a car, a bear, a house.

Uncountable nouns are nouns that denote things that cannot be counted. They require a singular verb. e.g. sugar, air, humidity.

Pair nouns are nouns that are used to denote a thing made of two parts which are the same. e.g. jeans, scissors, pajamas.

Group (or collective) nouns are nouns that denote a group of individuals. e.g. audience, family, staff.

Possessive nouns are nouns that indicate ownership (or possession). e.g. Lisa’s bag, my mother’s dress, lady’s gloves.

Compound nouns are nouns that are made up of two or more existing words. e.g. a rocking-horse, a father-in-law, a passer-by.

Countable/uncountable nouns

A countable noun can be either singular or plural. We can count desks. We can say a/one desk, two desks, three desks, etc. We NEVER leave a singular noun without a determiner.

Rules for making countable nouns plural:

1. Most nouns just add S.

e.g. a desk-desks

2. After -s, -ss, -sh, -ch, -x and -o we add -ES.

e.g. a tomato-tomatoes

BUT double o, abbreviations, musical instruments, proper nouns +S (e.g. videos, photos, pianos, etc.)

3. If a noun ends in a consonant + y, then we change y into i and add ES.

e.g. a study – studies

BUT we add just S if a noun ends in a vowel + y (e.g. a play-plays)

4. If a noun ends in -f, -fe, then we change -f,-fe into V and add -ES.

e.g. shelf-shelves

BUT roofs, chiefs, handkerchiefs, safes

Personal pronouns refer to yourself, the people you are talking to, or the people or things you are talking about. There are two sets of personal pronouns: subject pronouns and object pronouns. Object pronouns can sometimes be used in short answers (informally). The same thing happens after as and they.

A possessive pronoun comes before a noun, e.g. her pencil. We use an absolute possessive pronoun on its own, e.g. The pencil is hers.

There (as a pronoun) is used to introduce the subject of the sentence. To talk about the existence (or position) of something, we use there + be. Be agrees with the first following noun. We also use there with words like a lot of, many, much, more, enough and with numbers.

e.g. There is a book, three magazines and a wallet on the table.

It is used to introduce a thing, an action, a situation or an idea.

e.g. I’ve just watched an amazing film. It’s fantastic. (it=the film)

e.g. It’s Thursday today. It will be rather cold tomorrow.

e.g. There is a dog outside. It’s black and white.

We use it when we talk about the details.

Reflexive pronouns are used when you want to show that the object of a verb is the same person or thing as the subject of the verb.

e.g. She stretched herself out of the sofa.

Reflexive pronouns are not usually used with actions that people normally do to themselves, such as washing, dressing, shaving or changing clothes. So you do not usually say, for instance, He shaves himself every morning. You can, however, sometimes use reflexive pronouns with these actions for emphasis, or to talk about a surprising event, such as a child doing something that they were not previously able to do.

e.g. In spite of being a two-year-girl, Alice can dress herself rather quickly.

Some other verbs that do not take a reflexive pronoun are: afford, approach, complain, concentrate, decide, feel + adjective, get up, hurry (up), lie down, meet, remember, rest, relax, sit down, stand up, wake up, wonder, worry.

e.g. I myself sometimes say things I don’t mean.

Also the emphatic pronouns can mean ‘without help’.

e.g. She knitted this sweater herself.

Each other/one another (emphatic pronouns) are used to say that each person or thing does something to the other or others.

e.g. The women looked around at each other and smiled. Compare: The women looked at themselves (in the mirror) and smiled. (=Each woman looked at herself in the mirror.)

The pronoun one/ones

The pronoun one substitutes a singular noun while the pronoun ones substitutes a plural noun. We cannot use one or ones with an uncountable noun.

e.g. ‘I’ve got plenty of pens.’ ‘Could you lend me one (=a pen)?’

Sometimes we can leave one/ones after this, that, these and those; after each, another, which; or after a superlative.

e.g. We can’t stand this porridge. We would have that (one) right over there.

One/ones can be preceded by an adjective. If there is no adjective, we do not use a with one.

e.g. There is a bag lying on the sofa. It’s a green one.

‘What book would you like to read?’ ‘One with colourful pictures.’

One can refer to ‘people in general’ (including the speaker and the hearer).

e.g. One obeys (You obey) the laws of the country.

Indefinite pronouns are the pronouns that do not refer to a particular person or thing. With every, some and no, we can form words ending in one, body, thing and where. Everyone/everybody = all the people, everything = all the things, everywhere = (in) all the places, someone/somebody = a person, something = a thing, somewhere — (in) a place, no one/nobody = no person, nothing = no things, nowhere = (in) no places.

We use a singular verb after everyone, something, anything, etc. After words with one or body, we normally use they/them/their, even though the verb is singular. We can also use he, she, him, her, his, etc with someone/somebody when we know the person's sex.

e.g. Everyone is present.

Something rather strange has happened.

Nobody is driving their car today.

Someone drank their/his tea and left without paying.

Indefinite determiners (quantifiers)

Some/any go before a plural or uncountable noun. Usually we use some in positive sentences and any in negative ones (The same rule is applied to their derivatives – somebody, anything, etc.).

e.g. They have got some rice in the cupboard. Are there any mushrooms in the fridge?

Any can be used in a positive sentence (usually with if).

e.g. You can take any bus that goes to the city centre. (If there are any buses,…)

Much/many/a lot mean a large quantity. A lot of/lots of are preferable in positive sentences. Much and many are mainly used in negative sentences and questions. Much and many (but not a lot of) are used after too, so and as. We can sometimes use much and many in a positive sentence in formal English.

(A) Few/ (a) little mean a small quantity. The meaning of the structure is positive if there is an indefinite article. And if the article is missing, then the meaning is negative.

We can use all and most before a plural or an uncountable noun.

e.g. All children must study at school. Most trees have leaves.

We can use all (of), half (of), most of and some of when we talk about particular things or a restricted amount of something. We do not use them when we refer to things generally.

e.g. All of the children in this class must study English.

We can leave out of after all or half, but not before a pronoun.

We can also use all in mid position or after a pronoun.

e.g. The apples are all ripe.

We can use all with a clause to mean ‘everything’ or ‘the only thing’.

e.g. If you are keen on rock songs, listen to all I presented you. All he did was hide behind the tree.

We do not use all alone. Compare:

e.g. All the questions were solved. Everything was solved.

We can use most and some on their own.

e.g. I’ve read too many books about the revolution. Some of them are quite interesting, but most are boring.

We use no with a noun. We use none with of or on its own.

e.g. There is no water. None of us is ready.

‘I’d like a cup of tea.’ ‘But there is none left.’

We use every and each before a singular noun to talk about a whole group. Every can mean ‘all the people’, ‘everyone’, ‘anyone in the world’. Each can mean all the people seen as individuals, one by one.

e.g. Every person supported the Labour party. Each person signed the agreement.

Every means three or more, usually a large number. Each is more usual with smaller groups and can mean only two.

e.g. Every book has a plot. Each book has been written by this author.

We can use each (but not every) on its own or with of. We can also use each in mid position or after a pronoun.

e.g. There are several newly-appeared songs. Each is rather romantic.

She called them each.

We use whole before a singular noun. It means ‘all’ or ‘complete’.

e.g. We spent the whole night dancing.

We use both, either and neither for two things.

Both means 'the one and the other'. We can also use both in mid position or after a pronoun.

e.g. The government issued two laws, and both/both the laws/both of the laws/both of them concern the social sphere. The laws both concern the social sphere.

Either means ‘the one or the other’, and neither means ‘not the one or the other’.

e.g. He hasn’t answered either question/either of the questions/either of them.

Neither dress matches these shoes. Neither of the dresses/Neither of them matches these shoes.

We can use both, either and neither on their own.

e.g. We’ve got two cows, and both give a lot of milk/neither gives a lot of milk.

There are two basic types of questions in the English language:

Yes/No (Closed) questions are the questions that begin with an auxiliary or modal verb. They are answered with ‘yes’ or ‘no’. The answers can be short (yes/no + a pronoun + an auxiliary) or it is possible to use a full sentence.

e.g. ‘Were they playing checkers at 3 o’clock yesterday?’

‘Yes, they were.’/ ‘No, they weren’t playing checkers at 3 o’clock yesterday.’

Wh- (Open) questions are the questions that begin with a question word (what, where, when, why, how, which, whose). The number of answers is limitless.

e.g. Where are you flying?

Notes:

What is used alone or before a noun to ask about things.

e.g. What are you doing?

What colour shoes has he got?

What is also used to ask about people, animals or things when there is wide choice of answers.

e.g. What films do you like watching?

Which is used alone, or before nouns, one/ones or of (we do not use what before of and one), to ask about people, animals or things.

e.g. Which is her bike?

Which bike is hers?

Which one does Jane prefer?

Which of these books is the most interesting?

Which is normally used when there is only a limited choice of answers.

e.g. Which bag would you choose – the leather or the cotton one?

How is used alone or before an adjective/adverb.

e.g. How are you doing?

How long is the queue?

How high can he jump?

Other types of questions

Negative questions are formed with a negative auxiliary verb (in a full or contracted form). We use such questions in speech:

a) To ask for confirmation: Didn’t Tim prepare everything?

b) To express annoyance: Why can’t you be more polite?

c) To express admiration: Isn’t she a perfect singer?

d) To express surprise: Haven’t you heard how to pronounce this name?

Alternative questions are questions that express a choice of two or more possibilities.

e.g. Would you like a tea or a coffee?

Subject questions are questions we ask when we want to know the subject of the sentence. They usually begin with the words who, what, which. In such questions the verb is in the affirmative form (the auxiliaries do, does, did are not used).

e.g. John works in the shop. ‘Who works in the shop?’

Object questions are questions we ask when we want to know the object of the sentence. They usually begin with the words who, what, which.

e.g. John plays basketball. ‘What does John play?’

Tag questions are questions containing a structure consisting of an auxiliary verb followed by a pronoun, which is used at the end of a statement in order to form a question. They are mainly used in speech or informal writing when we want to confirm something or when we want to find out if something is true or not. If the statement is positive, the tag is negative and vice versa.

e.g. The shoes are black, aren’t they?

They haven’t arrived yet, have they?

Notes:

We use they to refer to nobody, somebody, everybody, etc.

We use positive tags after never, no, nobody, hardly, scarcely, little.

We use it in question tags to refer to nothing.

e.g. Nobody has come to the meeting, have they?

He knew little about physics, did he?

Nothing is impossible, is it?

Indirect questions are usually introduced with: Do you know…? Can/Could you tell me…? Have you got any idea…? I wonder/want to know…We use them when we ask for information politely. The word order in indirect questions is the same as in statements (subject + verb).

e.g. ‘Did she play the piano?’

I want to know if she played the piano (the indirect question).

ADJECTIVES

Adjectives are used to describe nouns. They have one form and do not change for singular and plural or before masculine and feminine nouns.

They can come:

1. before nouns:

e.g. She’s a wonderful girl.

2. after these verbs: be, become, seem, appear, look, feel, sound, taste, smell:

e.g. He’s become difficult to talk to.

Some verbs like look, taste, smell, appear, etc can be either linking verbs or action verbs.

e.g. She appeared happy. She appeared suddenly.

3. after get/make/keep/find + object

e.g. We found this task rather easy to complete.

But a few adjectives can go in one position but not in the other. Here are some examples of adjectives which can only go before a noun: main, indoor, only, former, chief (=main), elder (=older), eldest (=oldest), inner, outdoor, outer, principal (=main), upper.

e.g. Jane had always considered her looks her chief asset.

Here are some examples of adjectives which can only go after a linking verb: asleep, alike, pleased, ill, afraid, alone, ashamed, awake, alive, content (=happy), fine (=in good health), glad, unwell, well.

e.g. He seems to be quite content with a fairly minor role in the government.

There are some adjectives that we can use with the to talk about groups of people in the society:

1. to describe a social or economic position, e.g. the disadvantaged, the homeless, the hungry, the poor, the privileged, the rich, the starving, the strong, the unemployed, the weak.

2. to describe physical condition or health, e.g. the blind, the deaf, the dead, the disabled, the handicapped, the living, the sick.

3. to describe age, e.g. the elderly, the middle-aged, the old, the over-sixties, the under-fives, the young.

When we mean a specific person or a specific group of people, then we use man, woman, people, etc.

e.g. The elderly get some support from the government (=elderly people in general).

We saw the elderly people living in our neghbourhood at the theatre last night.

ADVERBS

We use adverbs with verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs. They tell us how, when, where and how often something happens.

e.g. Tim was crawling slowly.

The room is incredibly wonderful.

I take my responsibility here tremendously seriously.

We form many adverbs from an adjective + ly:

1. we do not leave out e, e.g. nice-nicely (BUT true-truly, whole-wholly);

2. -y changes into -ily after a consonant, e.g. heavy-heavily;

3. -le changes into -ly, e.g. possible-possibly;

4. -ic changes into -ically, e.g. dramatic-dramatically.

The ending –ly is a normal adverb ending, but a few adjectives also end in –ly: friendly, lively, lovely, elderly, likely, lonely, silly, ugly.

Some adverbs such as early, fast, hard, high, late, low, right, wrong, deep, long, near, straight are of the same form as adjectives.

Some adjectives form two adverbs with different meanings: free (=without paying) – freely (=uncontrolled), hard (=difficult) – hardly (=used for saying that smth is almost not true or almost does not happen at all), high (=large in size from the top to the ground) – highly (=very), late (=after correct or usual time) – lately (=recently), near (close to smth) – nearly (=almost), wide (=broad) – widely (=by a lot of people or in a lot of places).

Good is an adjective, well is and adverb. BUT we use well (even with linking verbs) to describe the condition of a person, his good health.

e.g. He is a good worker. He is good. (=Everything is ok. He has no troubles.) He is not well right now. (=He is ill.)

degrees of comparison

You can describe something by saying that it has more of a quality than something else. You do this by using comparative adjectives or adverbs. Not all adjectives have a comparative and superlative form nor can they all be graded. This is because there are two types of adjective, known as qualitative and classifying. Qualitative adjectives describe the qualities of a person or thing – whether they are large or small, happy or sad, etc. This type of adjective is gradable. We can use the intensifiers very, extremely with such adjectives.

e.g. a fairly smart girl, a very interesting book, an extremely short trip

Classifying adjectives place people and things into categories or classes. This type of adjective is non-gradable. We can use the intensifiers really, absolutely with such adjectives.

e.g. a daily newspaper, the western hemisphere, a really superb event

So, only qualitative adjectives usually have comparatives, but a few colour adjectives also have them. Adverb can also have the degrees of comparison.

THE GROUP OF PRESENT TENSES

Present Simple

We use the present simple:

1. For permanent states, repeated actions and daily routines.

e.g. She works at school. (permanent state)

She takes the bus to work every morning. (daily routine/repeated action)

2. For general truths and laws of nature.

e.g. The sun sets in the west.

3. For timetables and programmes.

e.g. The train from Moscow arrives at 3 o’clock.

4. For sports commentaries, reviews and narrations.

e.g. Peterson overtakes Williams and wins the race.

5. To give instructions or directions (instead of imperative).

e.g. You sprinkle some cheese on the pizza and then you bake it. (instead of: Sprinkle some cheese… )

The present simple is used with the following time expressions: usually, often, always, seldom, rarely, never, sometimes, every (day, week, month…), once/twice a week/a month…, in the morning/afternoon/evening, at night, on Mondays.

Note:

We often use the present simple with verbs which perform the actions they describe (performative verbs): admit, refuse, accept, acknowledge, advise, apologise, assume, deny, guarantee, hope, inform, predict, promise, recommend, suggest, suppose, warn.

e.g. I admit I can’t see as well as I used to.

We often prefer to use the present simple with verbs describing states.

e.g. I disagree with you.

The rules of forming the endings –s/-es:

1. We add –s to most verbs.

2. If a verb ends in -z, -ss, -sh, -ch, -x, -o, then we add es.

3. If a verb ends in a consonant + y, then y changes in i + es.

The rules of reading the endings –s/-es:

We pronounce it as [s] after a voiceless consonant, e.g. likes [laiks], sweeps [swi:ps].

We pronounce it as [z] after a voiced consonant and a vowel, e.g. plans [plænz], decides [di’saidz].

We pronounce it as [iz] after a fricative, e.g. watches [‘wotʃiz], kisses [‘kisiz].

The Present Continuous

We use the Present Continuous:

1. For actions taking place now, at the moment of speaking.

e.g. She is cleaning her teeth now.

2. For temporary actions going on around now, but not at the actual moment of speaking.

e.g. They are looking for a job these days. (not right now)

3. For changing or developing situations.

e.g. More and more people are going to the university these days.

4. With adverbs such as always, constantly, continually, forever etc. for actions which happen very often, usually to express annoyance, irritation or anger.

e.g. You’re always interrupting me when I’m talking.

5. For actions that we have already arranged to do in the near future.

e.g. We are moving into our new house next week.

The Present Continuous is used with the following time expressions: now, at the moment, at present, these days, this week, currently, just, still, nowadays, today, tonight, etc. Listen! Look!

The ending –ing:

1. most verbs+ing: go-going.

2. If a verb ends in –e, then we eliminate –e and add –ing: dance-dancing.

3. If a verb ends in –ie, we change –ie into –y and add –ing: lie-lying.

4. If a verb ends in a consonant+stressed vowel+consonant, we double the last consonant: swim-swimming, begin-beginning.

The Present Perfect

We use the Present Perfect:

1. For an action which started in the past and continues up to the present, especially with the state verbs such as be, have, like, know, etc. In this case we often use for and since.

e.g. Rachel has had the dog for three years.

2. For an action which has recently finished and whose result is visible in the present.

e.g. She has just washed her hair.

3. For an action which happened at an unstated time in the past. The exact time is not mentioned because it is either unknown or unimportant. The emphasis is placed on the action.

e.g. The Taylors have bought a sailing boat.

4. For an action which has happened within specific time period which is not over at the moment of speaking. We often use words and expressions such as today, this morning/evening, etc.

e.g. She has taken fifteen pictures today.

5. After such expressions as It’s/This is the first/the second time.

e.g. It’s the first time they have bought their own car.

Note: We use the present perfect to announce a piece of news and the past simple or past continuous to give more details about it.

e.g. The police have finally arrested Peter Duncan. He was trying to leave the country when they caught him.

The Present Perfect is used with the following time expressions: for, since, already, yet, always, just, ever, never, so far, today, this week/month, how long, lately, recently, still (in negations), this is the first/second time...

Have been to/have gone to/have been in

e.g. They have been to London. (They have visited London; but they are not there now; they have come back)

They have gone to London. (They have not come back yet; they are still in London)

They have been in London for 2 weeks. (They came to London 2 week ago and they are still there)

The Present Perfect Continuous

We use the Present Perfect Continuous:

1. To put emphasis on the duration of an action which started in the past, continues up to the present and is still going on, especially with such expressions as for, since, all morning/year.

e.g. Sam has been talking on the phone for half an hour.

2. To talk about repeated actions and changing situations up to now which may continue to change (recently, lately, over (the last decade), for some time).

e.g. They have been working hard lately.

3. For an action that started in the past and lasted for some time. The action may have finished or may still be going on. The result of the action is visible in the present (evidence).

e.g. Her feet hurt. She has been walking all morning.

The Present Perfect Continuous is used with the following time expressions: for, since, how long, lately, recently, over (the last decade), for some time.

THE GROUP OF PAST TENSES

The Past Simple

We use the Past Simple:

1. For actions which happened at a definite time in the past. The time is stated, already known or implied (past facts).

e.g. They went to the theatre last week.

2. For actions which happened immediately one after the other in the past (the succession of actions).

e.g. First, she paid the driver, then she got out of the taxi.

3. For past habits or states which are now finished. In such cases we can also use the expression used to.

e.g. Schools were/used to be very different a hundred years ago.

4. To talk about people who are no longer alive.

e.g. Michael Jackson sang great songs.

The Past Simple is used with the following time expressions: yesterday, then, when, How long ago…?, last night/week/month/Tuesday, etc., three days/weeks, etc. ago, in 1999.

The rules of forming the ending –ed:

1. +ed, e.g. want-wanted.

2. It the verb ends in a consonant+y, we change –y into –i and add –ed, e.g. study-studied.

3. If a verb ends in a consonant+stressed vowel+consonant, we double the last consonant, e.g. skip-skipped.

The rules of reading the ending –ed:

We pronounce it as [t] after a voiceless consonant, e.g. looked [lυkt], stepped [stept].

We pronounce it as [d] after a voiced consonant and a vowel, e.g. played [pleid], planned [plænd].

We pronounce it as [id] after the sounds [t], [d], e.g. wanted [‘wontid], demanded [di’ma:ndid].

To talk about something that was true in the past, but not true now, we can use used to + infinitive, would + infinitive. Used to describes past repeated actions and past states. Would describes only past actions, but not states.

e.g. He used to ride a bike a lot when he was 10. He used to love it.

He would ride a bike a lot when he was 10.

Would is more likely than used to when you describe more than one past action.

e.g. Each summer we would go to the seaside, we would swim and sunbathe all day long.

The structure be/get used to + gerund/noun describes the process of accustoming to something. It can be used in the past, present, or even future tense.

e.g. They were used to buying fresh vegetables.

I get used to driving fast.

The Past Continuous

We use the Past Continuous:

1. For an action which was in progress at a stated time in the past. We do not mention when the action started or finished.

e.g. At seven o’clock yesterday evening they were having dinner. (We do not know when they started of finished their dinner.)

2. For an action which was in progress when another action interrupted it. We use the past continuous for the action in progress (longer action) and the past simple for the action which interrupted it (shorter action).

e.g. He was walking down the street when he ran into an old friend.

3. For two or more simultaneous past actions.

e.g. She was talking on her mobile phone while she was driving to work.

4. To describe the atmosphere, setting, etc. in the introduction to a story before we describe the main events.

e.g. One beautiful autumn afternoon, Ben was strolling down a quiet country lane. The birds were singing and the leaves were rustling in the breeze.

The Past Continuous is used with the following time expressions: while, when, as, all morning/evening/day/night, etc.

The Past Perfect

We use the Past Perfect:

1. For an action which happened before another past action or before a stated time in the past.

e.g. They had already reserved a table when they went to the restaurant.

2. For an action which finished in the past and whose result was visible in the past.

e.g. He had broken his leg, so he couldn’t walk.

Note: The Past Perfect is the equivalent of the Present Perfect.

Time expressions: before, after, already, for, since, just, till/until, when, by, by the time, never.

The Past Perfect Continuous

We use the Past Perfect Continuous:

1. To put emphasis on the duration of the action, which started and finished in the past before another action or a stated time in the past, usually with since or for.

e.g. They had been skating together for 5 years before they entered the competition.

2. For an action which lasted for some time in the past and whose result was visible in the past.

e.g. She was really tired. She had been working hard that day.

Note: The Past Perfect Continuous is the equivalent of the Present Perfect Continuous.

The Past Perfect Continuous is used with the following time expressions: before, for, since, how long, until, etc.

FUTURE TENSES AND REFERENCES

Future Simple

1. In predictions about the future based on our opinion or on our past experience (usually with think, believe, expect, be sure, be afraid, probably, perhaps, certainly, etc.).

e.g. I’m afraid we won’t be on time for the meeting.

2. For on-the-spot decisions.

e.g. I’ll take this red umbrella.

3. For promises, threats, warnings, requests, hopes and offers.

e.g. Will you help me with this task? (a request)

Of course! I’ll explain it to you. (an offer)

4. For actions/events/situations which will definitely happen in the future and which we cannot control.

e.g. He will be 30 next year.

5. In a formal style: to talk about future events that have been previously arranged in some detail:

e.g. The meeting will begin at 11 o’clock.

Be going to

1. For intentions or ambitions that we have for the future.

e.g. I’m going to become a famous scientist one day.

2. In predictions when there is evidence that something will happen in the near future.

e.g. Look at the clouds! It’s going to rain.

Note:

We usually use the Present Continuous rather than be going to with verbs which express movement, especially go and come. We prefer going to with the verb be.

Time expressions used with the Future simple and be going to: tomorrow, the day after tomorrow, tonight, soon, next week/month, in a week/month, etc.

Present Continuous

We use the Present Continuous when we describe actions we have decided and arranged to do in the near future.

e.g. I’m flying to Paris tomorrow morning.

Present Simple

We use the Present Simple to refer to programmes and timetables.

e.g. This bus arrives in Liverpool at 7.30.

Note:

We use the Present Simple or Present Perfect, and not future forms after words and expressions such as while, before, after, until/till, as, unless, when, whenever, if, suppose/supposing, once, as soon as, as long as, by the time, in case, in condition that.

We use future forms:

a) With when when it is used as a question word.

e.g. When will they get married?

I’m not sure when they will visit us.

b) With if (=whether) when it is used after expressions which show ignorance, uncertainty such as I don’t know, I wonder, I doubt, etc.

e.g. I don’t know if/whether he’ll move house.

Other ways of expressing the future:

Be to + infinitive (formal English).

e.g. The President is to visit Poland next Monday.

Be about to + infinitive/be on the point of + -ing form (to refer to the near future).

e.g. Look! The bus is about to leave/is on the point of leaving.

Be due to + infinitive (timetables).

e.g. Their flight is due to arrive at 6:15.

Verbs such as decide, plan, intend, arrange, mean + to-infinitive (for plans or intentions)

e.g. We intend to buy a bigger flat.

Be sure to/be certain to/be bound to + infinitive (to express certainty about the future).

e.g. This plan is sure to/is bound to succeed.

The future in the past

1. was going to/was to/was about to/was due to + infinitive.

e.g. Mr Simon was going to resign.

2. was on the point of + ing.

e.g. They were on the point of leaving the house when the phone rang.

3. would

e.g. He mentioned that they would organize the quiz themselves.

The Future Continuous

We use the Future Continuous:

1. For an action which will be in progress at a stated future time.

e.g. This time next week we’ll be swimming in the Pacific Ocean.

2. We can use either the future continuous or the present continuous when we talk about planned activities or events in the future.

e.g. We will be leaving/are living for Paris tomorrow.

3. For an action which will definitely happen in the future as the result of a routine or arrangement.

e.g. Don’t call Jill. I’ll be seeing her later, so I’ll pass the message on.

4. When we ask politely about someone’s plans for the near future (what we want to know is if our wishes fit in with their plans.)

e.g. Will you be using the photocopier for long? (when you want to use it)

The Future Continuous is used with the following time expressions: this time next week, at 3 o’clock, still, for (a long time).

The Future Perfect

We use the future perfect to describe an action that will be finished before a future action or a stated future time.

e.g. She will have delivered all the newspapers by 8 o’clock.

The Future Perfect is used with the following time expressions: before, by, by then, by the time, until/till.

The Future Perfect Continuous

We use the future perfect to emphasise the duration of an action up to a certain time or action in the future.

e.g. By the end of next month, she will have been teaching for twenty years.

The Future Perfect Continuous is used with the following time expressions: for, all, by...for.

8Section VIIIThe passive voice

We form the passive with the verb to be and the past participle of the main verb. We can use the verb to get instead of the verb to be, especially in everyday speech.

The Present Perfect Continuous, the Future Continuous, the Past Perfect Continuous and the Future Perfect Continuous are not normally used in the passive.

The passive is used:

1) When the person who carries out the action is unknown, unimportant or obvious from the context.

e.g.: My car was serviced yesterday.

2) When the action itself is more important than the person who carries it out (in news headlines, newspaper articles, formal notices, instructions).

e.g.: The new hospital will be opened by the Queen in May.

3) When we want to make statements more polite.

e.g.: My new blouse is ruined. (instead of ‘You’ve ruined my new blouse.’)

Note: We use by + agent to say who or what carries out the action (a person, an animal, a natural phenomenon). We use with + instrument/material/ingredient to say what the agent used.

The Passive structures:

1. The structure with introductory it:

e.g.: It is thought that the Browns are the richest people in our heighbourhood.

2. Complex Subject (subject+passive verb+infinitive):

e.g.: He is believed to have bought that skyscraper.

3. Such structures as There is thought/said + to be…:

e.g.: There were said to be several markets in the centre of the city, but I never saw any.

4. Need + ing; need to be done.

e.g.: The windows need cleaning. The windows need to be cleaned.

Have smth done

We use have + object + past participle to say that we have arranged for someone to do smth for us. We can also use get instead of have, but it sounds rather informal.

e.g.: She is having her hair cut.

They had their house repaired last week.

9Section IXConditionals

Conditionals are clauses commonly introduced with if. Conditional clauses consist of two parts: the if-clause (hypothesis) and the main clause (result). When the if-clause comes before the main clause, the two clauses are separated with a comma.

Type 0 conditionals (general truth)

They are used to express smth which is always true. We can use when (=whenever) instead of if.

If + present simple, present simple.

e.g.: If you go by bus, you pay for a ticket.

Type 1 conditionals (real present and future)

They are used to express real or very probable situations in the present or future.

If + present tense, future/imperative/can/may/might/must/should/could + present bare infinitive.

e.g.: If you want to eat, you can order something from the café.

Type 2 conditionals (unreal present)

They are used to express imaginary situations which are contrary to the present and are unlikely to happen in the present or future. They are also used to give advice.

If + past simple/past continuous, would/could/might + present bare infinitive.

e.g.: If he trained hard, he could win the gold medal.

Note: we can use would in the if-clause when we make a request.

e.g.: If you’d like to come this way, the doctor will see you now. (=Please come this way…)

Type 3 conditionals (unreal past)

They are used to express imaginary situations which are contrary to facts in the past. They are also used to express regrets or criticism.

If + past perfect/past perfect continuous, would/could/might + perfect bare infinitive.

e.g.: If I had won the lottery, I would have bought a new car.

• We can form Conditionals by using words/expressions such as unless (Type 1 Conditionals), providing/provided that, so/as long as, on condition (that), what if, supposed/supposing, otherwise (=if not), but for, or (ever), even if, in case of/ in the event.

• We can use will or would after if to make a polite request or express insistence or uncertainty (usually with I don’t know, I’m sure). In this case if means whether. We can also use should after if to talk about smth which is possible, but not very likely to happen.

e.g.: If you will wait for a minute, Mr Blake will come to greet you.

(Will you please wait… - polite request)

e.g.: If you will continue shouting, I’ll send you out.

(If you insist on shouting… - insistence)

e.g.: If Jack should turn up, tell him to call me.

• We can use were instead of was for all persons in the if-clause of Type 2 conditionals.

e.g.: If Jack was/were kinder, he would have more friends.

We use If I were you… to give advice.

e.g.: If I were you, I would go to the party.

• We can use inversion in Type 1, 2, 3 Conditionals.

e.g.: Should he come tonight, tell him about that job.

Were Tom richer, he would buy a beautiful yacht.

Had they known about the film, they would have definitely watched it.

WISHES

I wish/If only can be followed by several different tenses:

1. I wish/If only + past simple/past continuous expresses an unrealistic desire for the present situation to be different (= I wish things were different now.)

e.g. Do you wish we lived nearer the school?

2. I wish/If only + past perfect expresses the desire that an action or event in the past had been different.

e.g. I wish she had come to my party.

3. I wish/If only + would expresses annoyance with the person or the situation and a desire for a situation to change, either now or in the future.

e.g. I wish Sheila would be more polite to her grandparents.

After the subject pronoun I and we, we use could instead of would.

e.g. I wish we could buy a bigger house.

11Section XIReported speech

We can report people’s words by using direct speech or reported speech. Direct speech is the exact words someone used. We use quotation marks in direct speech (‘’). E.g. ‘It’s wonderful,’ Mary said. Reported speech is the exact meaning of what someone said, but not the exact words. We do not use quotation marks in reported speech. We can either use the word that after the introductory verb (say, tell, etc.) or we can omit it. Personal pronouns, possessive adjectives/possessive pronouns change according to the meaning of the sentence. E.g. Mary said (that) it was wonderful. We can report someone’s words either a long time after they were said (out-of-date reporting) or a short time after they were said (up-to-date reporting).

REPORTED STATEMENTS

Verb tenses and time expressions change in reported speech:

1) when the introductory verb is in a past tense.

e.g.: ‘I’m moving to a new city next week,’ she said.

She said (that) she was moving to a new city the following week.

2) in out-of-date reporting.

e.g.: (Monday 4 April) Mary said, ‘I called the police to find my dog.’

(Wednesday 6 April) Mary said (that) she (had) called the police to find her dog.

3) when we consider what the speaker says to be untrue.

e.g.: ‘Sue lives in that house,’ Jack said.

Jack said (that) Sue lived in the house.

The tenses change as follows:

‘We need a new house,’ Tim said. Tim said (that) they needed a new house.

‘She’s cleaning the house,’ he said. He said (that) she was cleaning the house.

e.g.: this week – that week, these days – those days

e.g.: ‘This is a strange situation,’ Mum said.

Conditionals:

Type 1: present tense – past tense; will – would.
‘If you ask Tim, he will help you,’ he said. He said (that) if I asked Tim, he would help me.

‘If I woke up earlier, I would watch this show.’ ‘If I woke up earlier, I would watch this show.’

The verb tenses remain the same in reported speech when the introductory verb is in the present, future or present perfect.

The introductory verb is in the present tense:

a) When we pass on messages.

e.g.: Brian says he needs a car tonight.

b) When we report the content of a letter, an article, etc. while reading it.

e.g.: The notice says that the meeting starts in an hour.

c) When we refer to something someone says very often.

e.g.: Dad often says that I shouldn’t talk to strangers.

The verb tenses can either change or remain the same in reported speech.

a) In up-to-date reporting.

b) When reporting a general truth or law of nature.

e.g.: The teacher said, ‘Paris is the capital of France.’

The teacher said (that) Paris is/was the capital of France.

REPORTED QUESTIONS

Reported questions are usually introduced with the verbs ask, inquire, wonder or the expression want to know. The verb is in the affirmative (the direct word order). The question mark and words/expressions such as please, well, oh, etc. are omitted. The verb tenses, pronouns and time expressions change as in statements.

e.g.: ‘What did you make for dinner yesterday?’ Bob asked me.

Bob asked me what I had made for dinner the day before.

When the direct speech begins with a question word (who, where, how old, how long, when, why, what, etc.) the reported question is introduced with the same question word. When the direct question begins with an auxiliary verb (is, do, have) or a modal verb (can, may, etc.) then the reported question begins with if or whether.

e.g.: ‘Why do you want to leave your job?’ Pam asked me.

Pam asked me why I wanted to leave my job.

‘Do you like rock music?’ he asked us.

He asked us if/whether we liked rock music.

REPORTED COMMANDS, REQUESTS, SUGGESTIONS

To report commands, instructions, requests or suggestions in reported speech, we use an appropriate introductory verb (ask, order, beg, suggest, tell, etc.) and the to-infinitive, -ing form or that-clause depending on the introductory verb.

e.g. ‘Stop the car!’ the policeman said to him.

The policeman ordered him to stop the car.

‘Put all the ingredients in a bowl,’ she said to me.

She told me to put all the ingredients in a bowl.

‘Will you please hold this bag for me?’ Laura said to Helen.

Laura asked Helen to hold the bag for her.

‘How about going to the cinema?’ I said to them.

I suggested going to the cinema.

Infinitive is the basic non-finite form of the verb without an inflection. In English, this form of the verb together with the word ‘to’ in front of it is usually called a to-infinitive (e.g.: They decided to play volleyball tonight.) and the verb form on its own is usually called a bare infinitive (e.g.: He lets us stay there.).

e.g.: He’s got a headache. He claims to have been working on the computer all morning. (We emphasise what he has been doing all morning.)

The subject of the infinitive is omitted when it is the same as the subject of the main verb.

e.g.: He decided to move to England.

e.g.: We asked them to change the seat. (The subject of the main verb is ‘We’, whereas the subject of the infinitive is ‘them’.)

Participles

A participle is a word formed from a verb (e.g. going, gone, being, been) and used as a verbal adjective (e.g. working woman, burnt toast) or a noun (e.g. good breeding). In English participles are also used to make compound verb forms (e.g. is going, has been) or they function as verbs in non-finite clauses (e.g. He stole the bag containing $1000000).

The participles are:

a) Present participle (swimming, watching, etc.)

b) Past participle (done, washed, etc.)

c) Perfect participle (having taken, etc.)

Present and past participles can be used as adjectives. The present participle describes what somebody or something is (gives some outer characteristics, answers the question ‘What kind?’). The past participle describes how somebody feels (gives some inner characteristics, answers the question ‘How do you feel?’).

e.g. It was a very interesting film. (What kind of film?)

He was really interested in cinematography. (How did he feel?)

Participles can also be used as verbs:

• to express time.

e.g. After doing/having done her homework, she watched TV.

Having done her homework, she watched TV.

(=After she had done her homework, she watched TV.)

He broke his arm (while) playing hockey. (=He broke his arm while he was playing hockey.)

• to express reason.

e.g. Being late, Adam took a taxi. (=Because he was late, Adam took a taxi.)

Having spent all her money, Pam asked for a loan. (=Because she had spent all her money, Pam asked for a loan.)

• instead of a relative pronoun and full verb.

e.g. The man standing at the door is my boss. (=The man who is standing at the door is my boss.)

The information presented in the article was invaluable. (=The information which was presented in the article was invaluable.)

• instead of the past simple in narratives when we describe actions happening immediately one after the other.

e.g. Seeing the shadow, he screamed. (=He saw the shadow and he screamed.)

• to avoid repeating the past continuous in the same sentence.

e.g. She was climbing up a ladder carrying a bucket. (=She was climbing up a ladder and she was carrying a bucket.)

Glossary of grammatical terms

Adjective is a word used to tell you more about a thing, such as its appearance, colour, size, or other qualities.

Adverb is a word or phrase that modifies or qualifies an adjective, verb, or other adverb or a word group, expressing a relation of place, time, circumstance, manner, cause, degree.

Adverbial modifier tells us the time, place and manner of the action. It can be an adverb, adverbial phrase or prepositional phrase. A modifier answers the question When? Where? How? Every sentence does not necessarily require a modifier.

Agreement is the relationship between a subject and its verb, or between a number or determiner and its noun.

Alternative questions are questions that express a choice of two or more possibilities.

Auxiliary verb is one of the verbs be, have, and do when they are used with a main verb to make verb forms, negatives, questions, and so on.

Classifying adjectives are adjectives that place people and things into categories or classes.

Common nouns are nouns that denote a class of objects or a concept as opposed to a particular individual.

Comparative adjective or adverb is an adjective or an adverb with -er on the end or more in front of it.

Complement is a word that follows link verbs, such as be, seem, appear, become, get, feel, look, seem, stay, sound, which cannot be followed by an object. It completes the sense of a sentence by telling us something about the subject. It is expressed by an adjective, noun, noun phrase or a pronoun.

Compound nouns are nouns that are made up of two or more existing words.

Conditionals are clauses commonly introduced with if. Conditional clauses consist of two parts: the if-clause (hypothesis) and the main clause (result). When the if-clause comes before the main clause, the two clauses are separated with a comma.

Conjunction is a word used to connect clauses or sentences or to coordinate words in the same clause.

Countable nouns are nouns that denote things we can count. They can be singular or plural.

Determiner is a word used before a noun for showing which thing or things you are talking about (articles, possessives, demonstratives, interrogatives).

Emphatic pronouns are the pronouns that can coincide in the form with the reflexive pronouns. You can use them in addition to nouns or personal pronouns. You usually do this in order to make it clear or to emphasize who or what you are referring to.

Finite verb forms are verbs that show time or verbs which are limited by number, person and gender of the subject.

Gender is a grammatical term referring to the difference between masculine and feminine words such as he and she.

Gerund is a non-finite verb form functioning as a noun and ending in -ing.

Group (or collective) nouns are nouns that denote a group of individuals.

Indefinite pronouns are the pronouns that do not refer to a particular person or thing. With every, some and no, we can form words ending in one, body, thing and where.

Indirect object is a second object used with a transitive verb to indicate who or what benefits from an action, or gets something as a result of it.

Indirect questions are usually introduced with: Do you know…? Can/Could you tell me…? Have you got any idea…? I wonder/want to know…We use them when we ask for information politely. The word order in indirect questions is the same as in statements (subject + verb).

Infinitive is the basic non-finite form of the verb without an inflection.

Interjection is an exclamation, especially as a part of speech.

Linking verb a verb that links the subject and complement of a clause such as be,become, seem, appear.

Linking word is a word used to show relationships between ideas. It can be used to join 2 or more sentences or clauses (a clause is a group of words which contains a subject and a verb).

Modal verbs are the verbs that express some hues of actions; they also express the speaker’s attitude to the action indicated by the main verb.

Negative questions are the questions formed with a negative auxiliary verb (in a full or contracted form).

Non-finite verb forms are verbs that don’t show time or verbs which aren’t limited by number, person and gender of the subject (gerunds, infinitives, participles).

Noun is a word (other than a pronoun) used to identify any of a class of people, places, or things (common noun), or to name a particular one of these (proper noun).

Numeral is a word expressing a number.

Object is the part of the clause referring to the person or thing that is affected by the action of a verb. In English, the object is usually a pronoun, a noun or a noun phrase. Objects can be direct (directly involved in or affected by the action of the verb) and indirect (receive smth from or benefits from the action) ones. For example, Mary has showed me her house. ‘Her house’ is a direct object and ‘me’ is an indirect object.

Pair nouns are nouns that are used to denote a thing made of two parts which are the same.

Participle is a word formed from a verb (e.g. going, gone, being, been) and used as a verbal adjective (e.g. working woman, burnt toast) or a noun (e.g. good breeding).

Person is a term used to refer to the three classes of people who are involved in something that is said. They are the first person (the person speaking or writing),the second person (the person being addressed), and the third person (the people or things that are being talked about).

Personal pronouns are pronouns that refer to yourself, the people you are talking to, or the people or things you are talking about. There are two sets of personal pronouns: subject pronouns and object pronouns. Object pronouns can sometimes be used in short answers (informally).

Possessive nouns are nouns that indicate ownership (or possession).

Predicate is the part of the clause that follows the subject: it generally shows the actions of the sentence. Every sentence must have a verb. The predicate may be a single verb, or a verb phrase.

Preposition is a word governing, and usually preceding, a noun or pronoun and expressing a relation to another word or element in the clause.

Pronoun is a word used instead of a noun group for referring to a person, group, or thing that has already been mentioned or that is obvious from the context.

Proper nouns (or names) are nouns that are used to denote a particular person, place, or thing.

Reported speech is the exact meaning of what someone said, but not the exact words.

Qualitative adjectives describe the qualities of a person or thing – whether they are large or small, happy or sad, etc.

Question is a structure that typically has the verb in front of the subject and that is used to ask someone about something.

Reflexive pronouns are the pronouns that are used when you want to show that the object of a verb is the same person or thing as the subject of the verb.

Sentence is the smallest unit of speech conveying a thought and built in accordance with the laws of grammar.

Subject is the part of the clause referring to the person or thing that does or causes the action of a verb. In English the subject is usually a single noun, noun group or a pronoun, and comes before the verb.

Subject questions are questions we ask when we want to know the subject of the sentence. They usually begin with the words who, what, which. In such questions the verb is in the affirmative form (the auxiliaries do, does, did are not used).

Uncountable nouns are nouns that denote things that cannot be counted. They require a singular verb.

Verb is a word used to describe an action, state, or occurrence, and forming the main part of the predicate of a sentence, such as hear, become, happen.

Wh- (Open) questions are the questions that begin with a question word (what, where, when, why, how, which, whose).

Yes/No (Closed) questions are the questions that begin with an auxiliary or modal verb. They are answered with ‘yes’ or ‘no’.

e.g. They apologized to him for the mistake they made.

apply FOR a job / admission to a university, etc.:

e.g. She applied for the job she had ever dreamed about.

believe IN something:

e.g. I doubt that he believes in God.

belong TO someone:

e.g. This purse belongs to my daughter.

care ABOUT someone/something ( = think someone/something is important):

e.g. I don’t care about what you are saying.

care FOR someone/something:

like something (usually in questions and negative sentences):

e.g. I don’t care for ice-cream.

look after someone:

e.g. She is very old. She needs someone to care for her.

take care OF someone/something (= look after):

e.g. They took care of their nephew when he was a baby.

collide WITH someone/something:

e.g. There was an accident this morning. A bus collided with a car.

complain (TO someone) ABOUT someone/something:

e.g. We complained to the chief assistant about the quality of the leather bags they sell.

concentrate ON something:

e.g. Be quite! I’m trying to concentrate on reading.

consist OF something:

e.g. The university consists of several faculties.

crash/drive/bump/run INTO someone/something:

e.g. I accidentally ran into Mr Green in the downtown.

depend ON someone/something:

e.g. Your life depends on your attitude to the surrounding environment.

You can leave out on before question words (when/where/how, etc.):

e.g. ‘Shall I watch this movie?’ ‘It depends on how much you like horror films.’

die OF an illness:

e.g. She died of a heart attack.

dream ABOUT someone/something:

e.g. He dreamed about a round-the-world trip last night.

dream OF being something / doing something (= imagine):

e.g. We often dream of being kind.

also: ‘(I) wouldn't dream (of doing something)’:

e.g. ‘Don't tell anyone what 1said.’ ‘No, I wouldn't dream of it. ‘

happen TO someone/something:

e.g. What happened to the oil painting that hung on this wall?

hear ABOUT something (= be told about something):

e.g. Have you heard about the meeting next week?

hear OF someone/something (= know that someone/something exists):

e.g. ‘Who is Ellen Martin?’ ‘I have no idea. I've never heard of her.’

hear FROM someone ( = receive a letter / telephone call from someone):

e.g. They haven’t heard from their son for ages.

laugh/smile AT someone/something:

e.g. Stop laughing at this toddler! He is too small.

listen TO someone/something:

e.g. We spent the evening listening to records.

live ON money/food:

e.g. Mary’s salary is very low. It isn't enough to live on.

look AT someone/something (= look in the direction of):

e.g. Why is he looking at this paper so seriously?

also: have a look AT, stare AT, glance AT

look FOR someone/something (= try to find):

e.g. He’s looking for a new bike these days.

look AFTER someone/something ( = take care of):

e.g. We have to look after elderly people.

meet WITH someone ( = have a meeting with):

e.g. She met with her aunt at a nice café.

pay (someone) FOR something:

e.g. We pay for our flat every month.

but: pay a bill / a fine / $50 / a fare / taxes, etc. (no preposition)

rely ON someone/something:

e.g. You can’t rely on my brother. He is always telling lies.

Appendix 4

Verb + object + preposition

accuse someone of

e.g. The police accused her of robbery.

ask (someone) for (BUT ask a question)

e.g. They always ask us for help. The new student asked a lot of questions.

blame someone/something FOR something:

e.g. Everybody blamed her for the accident.

or: blame something ON someone/something:

e.g. Everybody blamed the accident on her.

We also say: "(someone is) to blame for something":

e.g. Everybody said that she was to blame for the accident.

borrow something FROM someone:

e.g. She usually borrows books from her neighbours.

charge someone WITH (an offense / a crime):

e.g. The women have been charged with shoplifting.

congratulate someone ON (doing) something (see also Unit 57b):

e.g. We congratulated our colleagues on completing the task.

divide/cut/split something INTO (two or more parts):

e.g. Cut the apple into four parts.

do something ABOUT something (= do something to improve a bad situation):

e.g. They should do something about their studies.

explain (a problem / a situation / a word, etc.) TO someone:

e.g. Can you explain this word to me? (not explain me this word)

also: "explain (to someone) that/what/how/why ... " (note the word order):

e.g. I will explain to you what I mean.

invite someone TO (a party / a wedding, etc.):

e.g. Why haven’t they invited us to their wedding?

leave (a place) FOR (another place):

e.g. Yesterday she left Moscow for London.

point/aim something AT someone/something:

e.g. You shouldn’t point this pen at you younger sister.

prefer someone/something TO someone/something (see also Unit 61):

e.g. They prefer jogging to swimming.

protect someone/something FROM (or against) someone/something:

e.g. He put suntan lotion on his body to protect his skin from the sun. (or

... against the sun.)

provide someone WITH something:

e.g. The alpinists are provided with all the equipment.

regard someone/something AS something:

e.g. I've always regarded you as one of my best friends.

remind someone OF someone/something (= cause someone to remember):

e.g. This place reminded us of our childhood.

but: remind someone ABOUT something (= tell someone not to forget):

e.g. It’s great that you reminded us about the deadline.

sentence someone TO (a period of imprisonment):

e.g. The robbers were sentenced to 3 years in prison.

spend (money) ON something:

e.g. She spends a lot of money on jewellery.

Note that we usually say ‘spend (time) doing something’:

e.g. We spend a lot of time reading books.

throw something AT someone/something (in order to hit them):

e.g. Someone threw an egg at the mayor while he was speaking.

but: throw something TO someone (for someone to catch):

e.g. Ann shouted "Catch!" and threw the keys to me from the window.

translate (a book, etc.) FROM one language INTO another language:

e.g. Her books have been translated into many languages.

warn someone ABOUT someone/something (of is also possible sometimes):

e.g. The police warned us about the fires in that area and we changed our rout.
